# Before and after

The brief was two lines: a team lead whose team of 8 cancelled every recurring meeting for 30 days and did it all in writing. The "before" is what a model wrote from that brief with no skill loaded. The "after" is the same post rewritten with this skill and no voice file, using only the points already in the before. Nothing new was added; the writer is made up.

## Before

```
Last month, my team of 8 cancelled every recurring meeting for 30 days.

Standups. Weekly syncs. The retro. All of it, gone.

Everything moved to writing instead.

I expected chaos. Here is what actually happened.

1. Writing exposed fuzzy thinking fast.
In a meeting, you can nod along to a half-formed idea. In a doc, a half-formed idea just sits there looking half-formed. People started thinking before they posted. The updates got sharper.

2. The quiet people got louder.
Some of my best engineers almost never spoke up in meetings. In writing, they were suddenly the ones with the most thoughtful takes. I had been missing their input for years.

3. Standups were mostly theater.
When we replaced the daily standup with a short written update, we lost nothing. The only real information in a standup is "I'm blocked." That takes one sentence in a channel, not 15 minutes of everyone's morning.

4. Decisions got slower, then better.
Async decisions took longer at first. But every one came with a written record of why we made it. No more "wait, why did we do it this way?" three months later.

5. The retro was the one we missed.
Honest feedback is harder to write than to say, and some things need a human face and a human voice. We ended up bringing back a retro, but shorter, and with a written pre-read so nobody wastes the first 20 minutes warming up.

6. Calendars stopped being Swiss cheese.
People got real focus time back. Nobody had a 30-minute gap between meetings that was too short to do anything useful.

What I'd tell another lead thinking about trying this:

Don't cancel meetings to be trendy. Cancel them to find out which ones were doing real work.

We came out the other side with fewer meetings, not zero. And every one that survived has a clear reason to exist.

The biggest lesson: a recurring meeting is a decision you made once and never revisited. Most of mine had quietly outlived their purpose.

When was the last time you audited your team's calendar?

If you're curious, I'm happy to share the template we used for the written updates. Drop a comment and I'll send it over.

#Leadership #Management #RemoteWork #Productivity #Engineering
```

## After

```
Last month my team of 8 cancelled every recurring meeting for 30 days (standups, weekly syncs and the retro) and did it all in writing instead.

The standup was the easiest to lose. The only thing anyone needed from it was who's blocked, and that fits in one line in a channel. It used to take 15 minutes of everyone's morning.

I also heard from people I almost never hear from. Some of my best engineers barely speak in meetings, and in writing they had the most thoughtful takes on the team. I'd been missing that for years.

Half-formed ideas got obvious too. You can nod along to one in a meeting, but in a doc it just sits there, so people started thinking before they posted.

Decisions were slower at first, but every one now has a written record of why we made it.

The retro is the one we missed. Nobody wanted to write down the honest stuff. It's back now, shorter, with a written pre-read so we don't spend the first 20 minutes warming up.

We ended the month with a lot less on the calendar, and I can say what every meeting that's left is for.
```

## What changed, and which rule caught it

- **Stacked one-line paragraphs for drama** ("Standups. Weekly syncs. The retro. All of it, gone.") became one sentence. Ban layer section 4, and verbless fragments that deliver facts, section 3.
- **A setup that promises a reveal** ("I expected chaos. Here is what actually happened.") is gone; the post opens on what happened. Section 2.
- **The numbered list with a bold label on every item** became plain paragraphs. A list is fine for steps; six labeled lessons is a template. Hot filter rule 4.
- **"Not X but Y" shapes** ("one sentence in a channel, not 15 minutes", "fewer meetings, not zero") were rewritten as plain statements. Hot filter rule 7.
- **Minted lines** ("Don't cancel meetings to be trendy. Cancel them to find out which ones were doing real work.", "a recurring meeting is a decision you made once and never revisited") were cut. Hot filter rule 6.
- **The colon reveal** ("The biggest lesson: ...") was cut. Section 3.
- **Engagement bait** (the closing question, the "drop a comment" offer) and **the hashtag block** were cut. Sections 2 and 6.
- **Contractions** are now consistent ("I had been" became "I'd been"). Hot filter rule 1.
- **A cliché** ("Swiss cheese") went with the paragraph it carried.

## What a voice file adds

The after is cleaner but still generic: it could be anyone. With a voice file (`reference/setup.md`), the skill also matches how this particular writer opens, how long their posts run, which habits they really have and which words they never use. That is the difference between "doesn't sound like AI" and "sounds like you".
