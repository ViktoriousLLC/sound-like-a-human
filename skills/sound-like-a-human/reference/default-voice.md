# The default voice

This is the voice of the person who made this skill, measured from their own writing done without AI: long-form writing and several years of posts. Every writer starts here. A writer who wants drafts to drift toward their own voice keeps a personal copy of this file as `voice-core.md` in their voice folder, and what the skill learns from their edits goes into "Personal habits" at the end. A personal habit always wins over a line above it. Full setup (`setup.md`) goes further and replaces these habits with ones measured from the writer's own samples.

Nothing here is about the author as a person. It holds habits, not facts, stories or numbers. Facts about the writer still come only from the writer (Hard rules in `SKILL.md`). Where a habit needs a detail, such as a sign-off about a weekend, use it only when the writer gave you that detail. Otherwise leave the habit out.

## How to use it

There are two tiers.

- **Baseline habits** are low-key. Follow them freely while drafting.
- **Signature moves** are the ones a reader notices. Allow at most 2 in a short piece (under about 400 words) and about one per section in a long one. That is a ceiling, not a target. A move belongs only where it comes naturally from what is being said. Never add one to make a draft sound more like the voice: a draft packed with signature moves reads as a parody, and the people who know the voice spot it right away. One real, concrete detail does more than three signature moves.

Each habit says where it fits. Outside those places, don't use it. The ban layer still applies in full, and the exceptions listed at the end are the only ones.

## Everywhere

Baseline:
- Zero em dashes and en dashes. Use commas, periods and parentheses.
- Exact counts, never rounded. When a number really is rough, say so ("about", "rough numbers").
- Numbers as digits. Use "+" only for a count that is still growing ("500+ entries"). At most 2 numbers in a short post.
- State the verdict. Don't hedge an opinion.
- Spell out an acronym if the reader might not know it. Skip that for one this reader uses every day.
- Firsthand detail: what was actually done, clicked, seen or measured.
- Criticise a practice, never a named person.
- Don't repeat other people's statistics unless the writer gives a source they checked.
- Read each sentence as if on a call. If the writer wouldn't say it that way, rewrite it.
- No made-up one-line slogans. No "not X, it's Y" in any form.

## Social posts

Pick ONE of these five kinds before writing, and keep its shape. Mixing two kinds is the most common failure.

1. **Quick reaction** to news: under 100 words, one thought, often no closer.
2. **Essay or list**: 120 to 400 words, a point plus something the writer lived through.
3. **Milestone or thanks**: name the people and what each one actually did. Write it as surprise that it happened, not as an announcement. Emoji are allowed at the high points.
4. **Share**: a link, an event or an announcement, one or two lines.
5. **Builder post**: something the writer built or used, with hands-on detail and their own usage numbers.

Measured shape (from the author's own posts):
- Length: median 122 words, with the middle half of posts between about 50 and 240. Some run 4 words, a few near 500. Vary it.
- Paragraphs: about 45% are a single line of 14 words or fewer. No paragraph runs past 4 sentences. Sentences run a median of 12 words.
- A bold first line on about 1 post in 5, mostly essays and milestones.
- A closing question on about 1 post in 4 overall: most essays end on one, quick reactions and shares almost never do.
- An essay often carries a numbered list of 3 to 7 items (lean past 3, since a tidy three reads as AI), each one a claim plus 1 to 3 sentences backing it.
- The openers that do best are a number from the writer's own life, a remembered first time, or a milestone. Use them only when the writer gave you that material.
- A post built on the writer's own number or usage beats commentary on someone else's product.

Baseline:
- Line breaks and short paragraphs. Lengths vary from post to post; don't settle into one size.
- A bold first line on some posts, not all.
- A review list can rate items with ✅ ❌ 🟡 at the start of each line.
- No hashtags.
- Sentences start with And, So or Plus where the writer would say them that way.
- Contractions, plus long sentences joined with commas and "and", the way people talk.
- End on something that fits this topic, or just stop. A question at the end only when the writer really wants the answers.
- Product reviews go in this order: what's good, the rough edges, then a wishlist.

Signature moves:
- A word doubled for emphasis ("really, really").
- One ALL-CAPS word for stress, never a whole shouted sentence.
- An exclamation mark when the excitement is real. "!!" at most once.
- "folks" for people.
- A short line on its own for time passing or a feeling, never for a fact.
- A comparison to a remembered time ("back when...").
- Teaching through a mistake the writer told you about, with specifics.
- A sign-off about life outside work, only from a detail the writer gave.
- One ellipsis for a trailing thought.
- Emoji only at real emotional high points, and rarely.

## Long-form (articles, newsletters, essays)

Baseline: everything under social posts except the post layout and the five kinds, plus:
- Name the phases of a long story, and close a long section with a 1 or 2 line summary. When sections close that way, the introduction doesn't preview them and the ending doesn't recap them.
- Lists inside a sentence as a) ...; b) ...
- Familiar idioms, used comfortably and not stacked.

Signature moves (in addition to the post ones):
- A quick question to yourself, answered straight away in the same sentence or the next.
- Saying the reader's doubt out loud as a full sentence, then answering it plainly.
- A burst of 2 or 3 questions to open a decision.
- A short made-up line of dialogue in quotes as an example ("Hey, can we move this to Friday?"), never set out as a labelled script.
- A comment on your own text ("more on that below").
- One plain image for belonging, used once, at the emotional peak of a story.

## Email and messages

Baseline:
- Contractions. Starting a sentence with And or So is fine. Use "Hence" as the more formal link.
- Spell out acronyms the reader may not know.
- Lists inside a sentence as a) ...; b) ...
- Match the energy of the moment: plain for routine, warm for thanks, no hype.
- One takeaway or one ask per email. Some facts just sit there.

Signature moves: at most one per email, and only in a friendly note. Never in a formal or cold email.

## Professional documents (memos, specs, reports, proposals)

Baseline:
- Everything under "Everywhere", with extra care on exact numbers, where each came from and firsthand detail.
- Spell out every acronym the first time, then use the short form from there on.
- Open with the target, then the result next to it, shown as a ratio or as before and after. (Made-up example: "Target: review time under 2 days. Result: 6 hours.") Never open with background.
- "I" for what the writer did (led, drove, escalated, launched), "we" for what the team delivered. Both can sit in one sentence. Only claims the writer gave you.
- The most common line is a bold label of 2 to 4 words, a colon, then one or two dense sentences.
- Parentheses carry the detail: counts, acronym expansions, how long something took. Give a claim its time span when the writer supplied one.
- Structure over prose. Lists can nest 2 or 3 levels. A plain paragraph only as a short summary at the top.
- Feedback or gaps: what was heard, then the action already under way.
- Flat statements. No hedges, no rhetorical questions, no question headers.
- Long comma-joined sentences next to short bullet fragments, with little in between. Don't smooth that out.
- Lists inside a sentence as a) ...; b) ... when the items are short.
- Emoji only as verdict markers at the start of bullets (✅ ❌ ⚠️). Never as decoration.

Signature moves: none, except one or two exclamation marks in a whole document, and only on a standout number.

## Slides

Baseline for every deck:
- Only the "Everywhere" rules for wording. Digits, exact numbers, no slogans.
- The header states the claim. "Sign-ups stall at the payment step" is a header; "Checkout" is a topic.
- Bullets are plain sentences. No hanging fragments.
- Ranges use "to": "3 to 5 taps", "$2 to $4 a month". Never a hyphen.
- At most one "so" or "which means" per bullet, and never two bullets in a row with the same logical shape. Some evidence just sits there; the header does the work.
- No counted header ("Three reasons..."). Use the points without the count.
- Each slide reads on its own without a speaker: no placeholder notes, no unexplained abbreviations, a screenshot on one card and the analysis on another.

Pick the deck's kind first, and keep to one.

**A pitch or proposal deck** asks a team or its leaders for a decision, and argues forward:
- Open an argument with three plain sentences: what the customer really pays for, what that means for the product, and what everything built has to protect.
- Prove a claim with a short firsthand story from the writer carrying one figure they can source. If it can't be sourced, keep the writer's exact figure and say where it came from ("from our own count").
- A new metric gets a name, its acronym expanded right away, one sentence on what it counts, and a link to a number the audience already tracks.
- Every estimate carries its caveat on the same slide ("rough numbers until we measure"). Actual results carry none.
- Show the math line by line with labels in capitals ("COST: ... MARGIN: ..."), never only the answer.
- Recommendations read as "Where I'd start". After the success metrics, list the guardrail metrics that must not get worse.
- Use the audience's own words for their metrics. Expand only terms they wouldn't use.
- Edge detail goes to an appendix.
- At most one question title in the deck.

**A story deck** is a look back at a finished project, shared with the team or the company, in the past tense:
- Beats in order: a title with a one-line tease, why it was worth doing, the setback after launch, the hardest problem, the target against the result, then one closing idea taught through a real two-way contrast. Leave out any beat the real story lacks. Never invent one.
- Each slide gets a short title plus one sentence under it that carries the verdict.
- The body is 1 to 3 short paragraphs, long comma-joined sentences next to very short ones.
- Numbers sit on one outcome slide as cards: the big number, what it is, a one-line caption, target beside result.
- "We" appears at the moments of most agency or most doubt.
- An open question goes in late, and only if the team really held it.
- To tighten, cut in this order: label scaffolding, cards for numbers the reader can work out, paragraphs tying the idea back to the product, then mechanics.

Signature moves: none. No "folks", no ALL-CAPS word, no ellipsis, no exclamation marks. Emoji only as verdict markers.

## Talks and scripts

Baseline: everything under social posts except the post layout, plus:
- Speech habits belong here and nowhere else: "right?" tagged onto a point, "I'm like..." to report a reaction, a self-correction partway through a sentence.

Signature moves: the post and long-form moves, at about the same ceiling per minute of speech as per short post.

## Words

- Use, where they fit: "folks", "long story short", "super" (as in "super useful"), "win-win", "really, really", "rinse and repeat".
- Still banned in generated text, even though people really say them: "at the end of the day", "pivotal". They read as AI in a fresh draft.

## Exceptions to the ban layer (only inside the places named above)

| Move | Allowed | Still banned |
|---|---|---|
| Quick self-question | with its answer in the same sentence or the next, in long-form and talks | as a drama beat ("Is X dead? No.") |
| Reader's doubt, then the answer | a full sentence of doubt, then a plain answer | a one-word "Wrong." punchline |
| Made-up dialogue | one short line in quotes | "Them:" / "Me:" scripts |
| A 1 or 2 line summary closing a section | long-form with named phases | an intro that previews plus a conclusion that recaps |
| Emoji on bullets | as verdict markers | as decoration |
| Bold label, colon, then a sentence | at the start of a line in professional documents | bold scattered through the body; a colon that sets up a reveal |
| Short lines on their own | time passing or a feeling | delivering a fact |

## Personal habits

Empty in the public file. In a writer's own copy, the rules learned from their edits go here, newest first, each with the date and the change that taught it. A personal habit wins over anything above it. When one contradicts a line above (the writer keeps cutting "folks", say), delete that line from their copy so the two never both apply.
