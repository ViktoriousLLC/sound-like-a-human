# The ban layer (organized by mechanic)

What never to write, what to ration, and how a writer's own habits override a generic list. Every mechanic appears once. Sections 1 to 4 apply to everything you generate, including notes written FOR the user. Sections 5 and 6 apply only to text written AS the user.

When you add a rule from a new outside list, add only mechanics that are not already here, filed under the right section. Never add a section per source; that is how ban lists bloat.

## 1. Banned words

Never in generated text: delve, foster, underscore (the verb), showcase, landscape (as metaphor), robust, leverage (the verb), seamless, actionable, unlock, empower, synergy, holistic, impactful, resonate, navigate (as metaphor), embark, tapestry, testament, boasts, garner, bolster, intricate, meticulous, vibrant, crucial, utilize, facilitate, streamline, cutting-edge, realm, beacon, multifaceted, elevate, supercharge, harness, ever-evolving, nestled, breathtaking, renowned.

Intensifiers models reach for and people rarely do: "genuinely", "certainly". If the writer's voice file shows they really use one of these, their file wins (section 7).

## 2. Banned phrases

- Padding dressed as insight: "it's worth noting", "importantly", "at its core", "in today's [anything]", "the reality is", "when it comes to", "let's unpack this", "make no mistake", "the real question is", "what really matters".
- Setups that promise a reveal: "Here's the thing", "The part everyone misses", "Let's dive in", "What nobody tells you", "Think about it:", "Here's what you need to know", "What if I told you", "What most people get wrong".
- Grandiose: "this changes everything", game changer, "represents a shift", paradigm shift, "a testament to", revolutionary, "this is huge", "reflects broader trends", "marks a pivotal moment".
- Social-post templates: "Agree?", "I'm humbled to announce", "Excited to share", "Let that sink in.", "Read that again.", "Unpopular opinion:", a bare "Thoughts?" as the closer, withholding the point to bait comments, keycap-emoji threads.
- Weasel hedges: stacked hedges ("could potentially", "may possibly", "arguably") and vague authority ("experts argue", "industry reports suggest"). A writer states a verdict and owns it. Labeling an uncertain NUMBER as uncertain ("rough numbers", "directional") is the opposite of hedging and is required.
- Assistant leakage that survives into the copy: "Would you like me to", "Let me know if", "I hope this helps".

## 3. Banned sentence shapes (these survive every word filter)

- Negative parallelism in every form: "This isn't about X. It's about Y.", "not just X but Y", "It wasn't the price. It wasn't the timing. It was the onboarding.", and the verbless verdict "A, not B."
- Verbless fragments that deliver facts: analyst-style closing lines, tidy summary fragments ("Three habits, one result."), a trait on its own as a sentence. Fragments are for feelings and passing time ("Nothing. Then more nothing."), never for facts.
- Balanced A/B seesaws: "The app handles the booking, so let me handle the people." Real rhythm is sequential and lopsided.
- Repeated consequence scaffolding: the same "so" / "which means" / "that's why" shape more than once in a short piece. Fix it by running the thought as one flowing sentence on commas and "and"s. Never fix it by splitting into short period statements, and never fix it by deleting content.
- Three-part flourishes of abstractions. Two items or four if it is a flourish; a list of concrete things can be any length.
- "X is Y until Z" miniatures, "the whole reason I..." justifications, "that's what makes it..." significance stamps.
- The tidy list with a twist ending ("...and none of them mattered").
- Clauses that claim a vantage point ("...so I got a front-row seat to how it really works"). Say what you saw.
- Colon reveals: "The best part: nobody had to approve it." (A how-to label that opens real instructions, like "Bring two forms of ID:", is fine.)
- Trailing "-ing" analysis: "...highlighting the team's commitment", "symbolizing", "reflecting". Name the concrete thing or cut the clause.
- Copula dodging: "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "features" where "is" or "has" works.
- Synonym cycling: the app, then the platform, then the tool. Repeat the right word.
- A staged question-and-answer as a drama beat ("Is remote work dead? No."). A quick self-question inside a sentence is fine if the writer does it.
- False ranges: "from strategy to snacks".
- Compressed abstract noun phrases and clipped phrasings: "help-seeking carried a cost", "attempt one" for "the first time we tried it".
- Theatrical openers: "Honestly?", "Look," as a pause for effect.

## 4. Banned structures

- Minted aphorisms and command-style maxim headers. Open with the concrete fact instead. A writer's own existing sayings may be quoted word for word, with their attribution; never invent new ones in their name.
- Titles and headers built as a two-part kicker ("The Garden Grew, And So Did The Bills") or a vague label ("Improving The Process"). A plain title that states what actually happened usually works best ("Most Of The Late Orders Came From One Supplier"), and it is fine for a title to give the answer away.
- Stacked one-line paragraphs for drama, and manufactured punchlines ("That's it. That's the post.", "Full stop.").
- Fake dialogue labeled "Them:" / "Me:".
- Fractal summaries (an intro that previews, sections that recap, a conclusion that recaps again). Say it once.
- A heading followed by a sentence that restates the heading.
- Writing about what changed ("now with improved onboarding") instead of describing the thing.
- Fixed recurring labels and recycled closers across pieces, and hooks that count inputs ("My 7 takeaways from 40 books this year").
- Every piece the same length. Real writers range from a few words to several hundred.

## 5. Register and energy tells (text written AS the writer)

- Zero contractions in a casual piece.
- The wrong emotional temperature (see hot filter rule 2).
- Emoji as decoration on every bullet, or in headings.
- Punctuation that is too correct. A real surprise can end "!!", a hesitation can be "...", a run-on can stay a run-on, each only where the feeling is real. Never invent typos; keep the writer's own when quoting them.
- Bold scattered through the body. Most writers bold the hook at most.

## 6. Dosage (things that are fine in small amounts)

Exclamation marks, emoji, bold hooks, question closers and hashtags are all real human habits; the tell is the dose. Take the writer's rates from their voice file. With no voice file, keep each one rare: at most a few exclamation marks per post, one doubled at most, no hashtags unless it is a campaign.

## 7. The writer's exceptions

Public slop lists flag some words and moves that a particular writer really uses. The writer's voice file lists those exceptions with counts from their own unassisted writing, and inside that file's stated scope they override sections 1 to 6. Outside that scope the bans apply as written. A word the writer uses in a story they lived ("a pivotal moment for me") can still be banned as generated filler ("marks a pivotal moment for the industry").

## 8. How a writer's own AI-assisted posts give themselves away

If the writer has past posts made with AI help, compare them with their handwritten ones. These patterns usually stand out:

1. Hooks that count inputs, where the handwritten ones open mid-scene.
2. One closer reused word for word across posts.
3. The same bespoke slot labels reused across posts.
4. Relayed third-party facts with no lived experience, several external stats per item.
5. Analyst-style closing lines, often verbless.
6. Every post inside the same narrow length band.
7. Pipeline scars (leftover dash-strip artifacts, odd characters). Diagnostic only.
8. Missing reflexes the writer always has, such as proposing a fix or reaching for a personal analogy.
9. Engagement that decays post after post.
